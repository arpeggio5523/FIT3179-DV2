Dogs in Australia

Which dogs share Australian homes, where are they recorded, and how are preferences changing?

Read each dataset separately. National surveys, council registrations and RSPCA records cover different places, dates and populations. Their totals should not be added together.

01 · Dogs at home

From size to local favourites

Size describes the dogs sharing homes nationally. South Australian records offer a closer look at breed preferences.

Australia · 2025 survey

Small and medium dogs account for 76%

Small and medium dogs each make up 38% of the published Australian size distribution. Dogs weighing more than 25 kg account for the remaining 24%.

Highlight size

Each square represents one percentage point of the published size distribution.

Size survey — These are survey estimates, not registration counts. The squares do not represent 100 individually surveyed dogs. Size is based on weight, not breed; some small dogs may be puppies. Weight categories retain the report’s wording.

Data: (Animal Medicines Australia, 2025)

South Australia · May 2025

Kelpies lead South Australia’s recorded breeds

Australian Kelpie and Border Collie lead with 18,107 and 17,353 dogs. Their gap is 754 records, compared with 4,289 between Border Collie and third-placed Labrador Retriever.

SA breed counts — Dogs and Cats Online (DACO) records use the status “At Home”. This is a snapshot of recorded dogs, not new registrations during the year.

Data: (Dog and Cat Management Board, 2025b)

South Australia · 68 councils

Border Collies are favourites across more councils

Border Collies appear in the top five of 63 of 68 councils. Australian Kelpies and Maltese Crosses each appear in 47. This measures how widely breeds feature among local favourites.

The breed with the highest statewide count does not necessarily appear among the favourites in the most councils.

Council treemap — Each council contributes five appearances, giving 340 in total. Rectangle area represents a share of those appearances, not a percentage of dogs. Each ranking position counts equally. Interstate Council and Outback Communities Authority are excluded.

Data: (Dog and Cat Management Board, n.d.)

02 · Dogs on the map

Location tells more than one story

Counts reveal where dog records are concentrated. Proportions answer a different question: where is owning several dogs more common?

City of Casey · August 2024

Berwick has the most dog records in Casey

Casey’s dataset contains 27,618 dog records across 29 suburbs. Berwick records 4,971 dogs compared with 2,425 in Narre Warren South. The largest circle identifies where recorded totals are concentrated.

Larger circles represent more dog records. Hover over a suburb for its count.

18.0%of Casey’s recorded dogs are in Berwick
2.05×as many as Narre Warren South

Casey symbol map — Circles mark representative suburb locations, not individual animals. These counts do not measure dogs per resident. Records have a last modification date of 28 August 2024.

Data: (City of Casey, n.d.-a; City of Casey, n.d.-b)

Townsville · March 2026

Townsville’s records span ten divisions

Townsville recorded 26,034 dogs in March 2026. Division 1 had the most records, with 3,332, compared with 2,005 in Division 3.

One dot represents about 50 dog records; dots show division-level distribution, not addresses.

Townsville dot map — Dots are randomly allocated within each division, with totals rounded to the nearest dot. They are not actual addresses and are not restricted to residential land. Interpret the pattern at division level.

Data: (Townsville City Council, 2026; Townsville City Council, n.d.)

South Australia · May 2025

Multiple-dog ownership varies between councils

Among recorded dog owners, 41.9% in Adelaide Plains had two or more dogs, compared with 12.5% in the City of Adelaide.

Using a percentage allows councils with different numbers of recorded owners to be compared.

SA ownership map — Percentage = owners with two or more dogs ÷ owners with at least one dog × 100. These are owner records, not households or all residents. Council refers to the owner’s residence. Interstate and Outback Communities Authority records are excluded.

Data: (Dog and Cat Management Board, 2025a; Australian Bureau of Statistics, 2025)

03 · Changing preferences

Rank and count tell different stories

A breed’s rank shows its position relative to others. Its count reveals the size of the recorded change.

Dogs Australia · 2018–2025

All seven groups finish below their 2018 totals

In Dogs Australia’s national registration series, Non-Sporting had the largest percentage decline between 2018 and 2025, at 56.1%. Gundogs had the smallest decline, at 15.0%.

Registration heatmap — Each cell compares a breed group with its 2018 total. Orange indicates a decrease; blue indicates an increase. These are Dogs Australia national breed registrations, not council registrations or all Australian dogs. Hover for counts and percentages.

Data: (Dogs Australia, n.d.)

Townsville · March 2023–2026

Miniature Dachshund climbs from 32nd to 13th

Maltese Terrier Cross remained first across Townsville’s March snapshots from 2023 to 2026. Miniature Dachshund climbed 19 places, while Dachshund moved from 15th to 12th.

Rank 1 is highest. Six breeds are highlighted; grey lines provide context. Hover for counts.

Townsville rankings — The display groups ranks worse than 35 at the lower boundary. Registration records do not necessarily equal the actual dog population.

Data: (Townsville City Council, 2026)

Townsville · March 2023 and 2026

The rise in rank comes with more recorded dogs

Miniature Dachshund increased from 268 to 460 records, a rise of 71.6%. Staffordshire Bull Terrier fell by 299 records, or 22.9%.

Townsville count comparison — These six selected breeds are not the complete breed list. Crossbreeds retain their published categories. The endpoints do not show changes between the two dates.

Data: (Townsville City Council, 2026)

04 · Dogs in care

Beyond registration: finding a home

RSPCA records add another perspective: the outcomes and year-end care status of dogs in its facilities.

RSPCA societies · 2024–2025

Rehoming is the largest recorded category

Rehoming accounts for 6,839 of 15,852 dog records, or 43.1%. Follow the ribbons from each society to its recorded outcomes and year-end care status.

These figures describe RSPCA care records. They do not track the dogs shown in the council charts above.

RSPCA outcomes — “Received” includes animals in facilities during the financial year, not only new admissions. “Currently in care” includes foster care at year end. “Other” includes deaths in care. Reclaimed and returned remain separate as published.

The report states that ACT, NT and TAS exclude pound animals, while the other states include them. Coverage differs, so these totals should not be used to rank society performance. They do not represent every Australian shelter.

Data: (RSPCA Australia, 2025)
